title: Writing good cards
group: cards
evidence: practice
summary: One fact per card, a clear question, and an answer you can check at a glance.

A good card takes a few seconds to review and is hard to get wrong by accident. Most trouble with spaced repetition comes from cards that are too big or too vague.

## Rules that matter most

- Understand first. Make cards from things you understand. Memorized words without meaning fall apart.
- One fact per card. If the answer has several parts, you will often miss one, and the whole card counts as forgotten. This is the minimum information principle.
- Ask one clear question. Only one answer should fit. If two could, add the context that decides.
- Keep the answer short. You should be able to check it in a glance.
- Avoid sets and long lists. "Name all EU members" is a poor card. See [lists and multicards](/learn/lists).
- Make similar cards differ. See [interference](/learn/interference).
- Add something personal. An example from your life, a place, a joke. It gives the memory a hook.

## How AiNKI helps

While you write, AiNKI points out a long list on the back and cards that look like this one. Later it flags cards that keep slipping or ask too much at once, and offers to split them.

## Sources

- Wozniak, P. (1999). Effective learning: Twenty rules of formulating knowledge. supermemo.com

These rules come from decades of experience with spaced repetition rather than from controlled trials. They match what the research on [interference](/learn/interference) and [testing](/learn/testing) would predict.
